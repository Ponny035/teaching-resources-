Game Theory & Externalities

When your best move depends on what someone else does, you’re in a game. And when your choices hurt (or help) people who aren’t part of the deal, markets get the quantity wrong. Both are about interdependence — and both can be fixed by changing incentives.

5A · Payoff matrix: find the Nash equilibrium

Two players choose at the same time. Each cell shows A’s payoff, B’s payoff. A highlighted number means “this is the best reply to what the other player does”. A cell where both numbers are highlighted is a Nash equilibrium: no one wants to change alone. Pick a classic game — or type your own payoffs.

What the analysis says

    Things to try
    • Prisoner’s dilemma: both players have a dominant strategy (Confess), so the Nash equilibrium is (Confess, Confess) — yet both would be better off at (Silent, Silent). Individually rational, collectively bad.
    • Change the payoffs for the mutual-cooperation cell so it’s lower than the temptation payoff — see when cooperation stops being an equilibrium.
    • Stag hunt and Battle of the sexes have two pure equilibria. Which one gets played? Game theory alone can’t say.
    • Matching pennies has no pure equilibrium — only a mixed one where each player randomises.

    5B · Repeated play & collusion: can cooperation survive?

    Cartels face a prisoner’s dilemma: each member gains by cheating on the deal (Defect), but if everyone cheats, all are worse off. When the game is repeated, the threat of future punishment can sustain collusion (Cooperate). Play 10 rounds against a computer opponent. Payoffs: both cooperate 3 each · both defect 1 each · you defect on a cooperator 5 vs 0.

    Things to try
    • Against tit-for-tat, cooperate every round: you both get 30. Try defecting once — you gain 2 that round but lose more later.
    • Against “always cooperate”, defecting every round is the best response — cooperation only survives if the opponent punishes.
    • Defect on the last round against tit-for-tat. Because the end is known, backward induction says both should defect in the last round — and then the one before, and so on. Real cartels survive because there is no known last round.

    5C · Externalities: when private and social costs differ

    An externality is a cost or benefit that lands on a third party. Producers weigh only private costs, so the market quantity is too high with a negative externality (pollution) and too low with a positive one (vaccination). The efficient quantity is where marginal social benefit = marginal social cost. A Pigouvian tax (or subsidy) equal to the external effect fixes it. Drag the demand or supply curve, set the externality and try a policy.

    Things to try
    • Negative externality: increase the external cost per unit. The social cost curve rises above the private supply, the efficient quantity falls, and the deadweight loss triangle grows.
    • Add a tax and slide it up from zero. Deadweight loss shrinks until the tax equals the external cost per unit — then too much tax makes it grow again.
    • Switch to a positive externality (e.g. vaccines) — the fix is a subsidy, not a tax.
    • Steeper supply (less responsive) means the same tax changes quantity less — but the optimal tax is still the external cost.

    Key takeaways

    • A dominant strategy is best whatever the other player does. A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where nobody can gain by switching alone.
    • In a prisoner’s dilemma the Nash equilibrium is inefficient. Cartels are unstable because each member wants to cheat — unless repeated interaction makes punishment credible.
    • Externality = a cost or benefit imposed on people outside the transaction. Markets ignore it: MPC ≠ MSC or MPB ≠ MSB.
    • Pigouvian tax = marginal external cost; subsidy = marginal external benefit. It makes people face the true social price.

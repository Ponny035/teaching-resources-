Economic Thinking & Consumer Choice

Every choice is a trade-off. This session builds the one picture that the rest of microeconomics stands on: what you can afford (the budget line) versus what you want (indifference curves), and where the two meet.

  • Scarcity
  • Opportunity cost
  • Marginal thinking
  • Preferences & utility
  • Budget constraint

1A · Budget line, indifference curve & the best bundle

Scarcity means your income is limited, so getting more of one good means getting less of another. The slope of the budget line is the opportunity cost. Your tastes are drawn as an indifference curve — every bundle on it makes you equally happy. Drag the white dot to choose a bundle.

Things to try
  • Drag the dot to a point inside the shaded triangle. Is there always a better bundle? (More is better, so yes — move to the line.)
  • Drag it slowly along the budget line. Watch “utility per $” for each good — the highest utility happens where they’re equal. That’s marginal thinking.
  • Raise the price of pizza. The line pivots — the opportunity cost of pizza (in movies) goes up.
  • Change “taste for pizza”. The best bundle moves along the line towards the good you like more.

1B · Price changes & taxes: income effect vs. substitution effect

A price change makes you buy differently for two different reasons. (1) Substitution effect: the good is now cheap or dear relative to others, so you swap. (2) Income effect: the same money now buys more or less, so you’re effectively richer or poorer. Economists split the move A → B via an in-between bundle C on the original indifference curve. Switch to Per-unit tax / subsidy to see the same idea with a government: the tax raises the price, the government collects revenue, and part of your loss is deadweight loss.

Things to try
  • Slide the new price down from $6 to $3. A→C is the substitution effect (moving along the original curve to the new price ratio). C→B is the income effect (moving to a higher curve).
  • Now raise the price above $6 — both effects reverse.
  • Switch to Per-unit tax and slide it up. Your loss splits into what the government collects (a transfer) and the deadweight loss (waste). Try “Rebates it lump-sum”: B′ lands on the old budget line — almost only the substitution effect remains, and you’re still worse off than at A.
  • Slide the tax below zero to make it a subsidy: you gain, taxpayers pay more than you gain, and the gap is deadweight loss.
  • Raise “taste for pizza” (pizza becomes a bigger share of your budget). The income effect grows — a price cut matters more for a good you spend a lot on. When pizza is a small part of the budget, most of the response is substitution.

1C · Labour supply: the trade-off between hours worked (L) and the wage

A worker has a fixed number of usable hours and splits them between leisure and work. The wage is the opportunity cost of leisure: the slope of the budget line. When the wage rises there are two forces. The substitution effect says leisure is now dearer, so work more. The income effect says you are richer, so buy more leisure and work less. Which wins decides whether the labour supply curve slopes up or bends backward. On the right, the supply curve is traced over all wages. Time-saving technology gives the worker more usable hours and shifts the supply curve.

Labour supply curve: hours vs. wage

Things to try
  • Slide the new wage up from $4 towards $40. Hours first rise (substitution effect), peak around $10, then fall (income effect) — the curve bends backward. Watch the A→C and C→B arrows change size.
  • Raise non-labour income: the income effect gets stronger, the worker works fewer hours overall, and the bend arrives earlier.
  • Increase the taste for leisure so much that the worker doesn’t work at low wages: that is the reservation wage (the wage needed to give up the first hour of leisure).
  • Add time-saving technology: the whole supply curve shifts right (more hours offered at every wage), and the dashed line shows where it was.

Key takeaways

  • Opportunity cost = what you give up. On the budget line it equals the price ratio, Px / Py.
  • At the best bundle the indifference curve just touches the budget line: MRS = Px / Py, or equivalently MUx / Px = MUy / Py (equal “bang per buck”).
  • If MRS > Px/Py you value X more than the market asks — buy more X, less Y. Reverse if less.
  • A price change = substitution effect (relative prices) + income effect (purchasing power).
  • Labour supply: the wage is the opportunity cost of leisure. A higher wage has a substitution effect (work more) and an income effect (buy more leisure). If the income effect wins the supply curve bends backward. Time-saving technology shifts it right.
  • A per-unit tax works the same way: what consumers lose splits into tax revenue (a transfer to the government) and deadweight loss (the substitution effect makes people give up trades that were worth it).

Market Power & Market Structures

In perfect competition no one can set the price. What changes when a firm can? It restricts output, charges above marginal cost, and society loses. Here you switch between market structures on the same demand curve and compare who gets what.

4A · One demand curve, five market structures: short run vs. long run

A firm with market power faces the whole downward-sloping demand curve. To sell one more unit it must cut the price on all units, so marginal revenue (MR) lies below demand. It picks output where MR = MC, then charges what buyers will pay. Choose a structure and compare the short run (left: capacity and number of firms fixed) with the long run (right: firms can enter, exit and adjust) side by side. Drag the demand curve or the MC line in either chart. Add a per-unit tax or subsidy to see who pays it under each structure.

Things to try
  • Perfect competition: demand starts with a boom. In the short run price jumps up the steep supply curve and firms earn profit (green box); in the long run entry pushes price back to LRAC and profit vanishes. Drag demand back to 100 and the two runs match.
  • Monopoly: short run = long run. Barriers to entry let the profit last, and the red triangle (deadweight loss) never goes away.
  • Oligopoly (Cournot): the short run has a fixed number of firms. In the long run entry changes the number of firms until profit per firm is ~0. Raise fixed cost F: fewer firms survive.
  • Monopolistic competition: profit in the short run, but new brands shift each firm’s demand left in the long run until it just touches ATC (zero profit), with price still above MC.
  • Tax pass-through: add a per-unit tax. A monopolist with linear demand raises price by only about half the tax; a competitive industry with free entry passes on all of it in the long run. The gold box is government revenue.
  • Perfect price discrimination: output is efficient (no DWL) but consumers get zero surplus.
  • Raise the marginal cost line. The monopoly price rises by half as much — why? (MR slopes twice as steeply as demand.)

4B · Price discrimination: different prices for different people

If a firm can split customers into groups with different demand (students vs. adults, economy vs. business), it sets MR = MC separately in each group. The group with the less elastic demand pays more. Compare profits with a single price to profits with two prices. Drag a demand curve to shift it.

Group 2: less price-sensitive customers (adults)

Things to try
  • Make the two demand curves identical. The two prices coincide and price discrimination earns nothing extra.
  • Make group 1 much more elastic (flatter). The gap between the prices widens and the profit gain rises.
  • Watch total surplus: discrimination can even raise efficiency when it lets the firm serve a group it would otherwise ignore.

4C · Monopsony: market power on the buying side

A monopsony is a single buyer — e.g. the only big employer in a town. To hire one more worker it must raise the wage for everyone, so the marginal cost of labour (MCL) lies above the supply curve. The firm hires where MRP = MCL but pays only what workers will accept, so employment and wages are below the competitive level. Add a minimum wage and see something surprising, or a payroll tax / wage subsidy to see who bears it.

Things to try
  • Switch between competitive and monopsony employers to see the drop in wage and employment.
  • In monopsony, raise the minimum wage from $0. Between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage, employment rises.
  • Add a payroll tax per worker: the employer’s demand for labor shifts down by the tax, the wage falls and employment drops. The gold area is government revenue; workers bear the share the wage falls by.
  • Push the minimum wage above the competitive wage — now employers cut jobs and unemployment appears, as in the standard competitive model.

Key takeaways

  • Market power means P > MC. A monopolist sets MR = MC, then reads the price off demand.
  • Restricting output below the competitive level creates deadweight loss, transfers surplus from consumers to the firm, and lowers total welfare.
  • More competitors (oligopoly → competition) push price toward MC. Perfect price discrimination removes DWL but takes all consumer surplus.
  • Third-degree price discrimination charges more to the less elastic group.
  • Monopsony mirrors monopoly: it under-hires and underpays relative to the competitive outcome; a modest minimum wage can raise employment.

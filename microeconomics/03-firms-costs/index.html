3A · One price-taking firm: cost curves and the profit-maximising output

In perfect competition the firm is a price taker: it can sell as much as it likes at the market price, so marginal revenue (MR) = price. It keeps producing while the next unit adds more revenue than cost, and stops where MR = MC. Drag the price line up and down. The shaded box is profit (green) or loss (red).

Things to try
  • Too many lines? Switch Detail level to Simple: ATC only. It hides AVC, AFC and the fixed/variable cost sliders so you can focus on ATC, MC and the price line.
  • Raise the price and see the output slide up along the MC curve. That’s why MC (above the shutdown point) is the firm’s supply curve.
  • Drop the price below the ATC minimum: the firm makes a loss but should still produce if price ≥ AVC (it covers some fixed costs). Below AVC, shut down and lose only the fixed cost.
  • In the total view, the profit gap is largest where the dashed line (slope = MC) is parallel to the revenue line (slope = P). That’s the same MR = MC rule seen another way.
  • Raise fixed cost: ATC moves up but MC doesn’t — so the output decision is unchanged; only profit falls. (Fixed costs are sunk.)

3B · The whole market: how competition erodes profit

Market supply is the sum of all firms’ MC curves (above shutdown). If firms make profit, others enter: supply shifts right, price falls. If they make losses, firms exit. It stops at zero economic profit where P = minimum ATC. Try a demand boom, then press Auto-run. You can also add a per-unit tax or subsidy on the firms.

The market

A typical firm

Things to try
  • Set very few firms: the price is high and each firm earns a fat profit. Press “Auto-run” and watch entry push price down.
  • Press “Reset”, then increase market demand. Short-run profits appear → entry → in the long run price returns to minimum ATC, but with more firms and more output.
  • Set too many firms and watch exit happen.
  • Add a per-unit tax on firms. Supply shifts up by the tax: consumers pay more, firms keep less, and the government collects the gold box. Press “Auto-run”: in the long run entry and exit push what firms receive back to the minimum ATC, so consumers end up paying min ATC + tax — the whole tax is passed on.

3C · Short run vs. long run: how does the price affect one firm?

The short run firm is stuck with its plant, so it slides along its short-run curves (SRMC, SRATC). In the long run it can choose the best plant size for each output, so its cost curves are the flatter LRAC and LRMC, and each SRATC just touches LRAC at one point. Below, the same market price hits both firms side by side. Drag the price line in either chart.

Things to try
  • Set the price above the dashed level ($9). Both firms earn profit, but in the long run entry pushes the price back down to the minimum of LRAC, where profit is zero.
  • Watch the “Output gained if price +$1” row: the long-run firm responds more, because its LRMC is flatter than its SRMC. That’s why long-run supply is more elastic than short-run supply.
  • In the long-run chart, the dashed violet curve is the SRATC of the plant the firm picks. It touches LRAC exactly at the chosen output — LRAC is the lower envelope of all the short-run curves.
  • Push the price below the dashed level: the short-run firm may keep producing (if P ≥ AVC), but in the long run it must exit.

Key takeaways

  • Short run = some inputs (plant) fixed; long run = everything can change, so LRAC and LRMC are flatter and long-run supply is more elastic.
  • Fixed costs don’t change with output; variable costs do. ATC = AFC + AVC. MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums.
  • Profit-maximising rule for any firm: produce where MR = MC (and only if P ≥ AVC).
  • For a price-taker MR = P, so P = MC. The firm’s supply curve is MC above min AVC.
  • Short-run profit invites entry; in the long run P = min ATC and economic profit is zero. Zero economic profit still means normal returns to the owners.

Demand, Supply, Equilibrium & Elasticity

Markets bring buyers and sellers together. Here you build the curves from individual willingness-to-pay, then drag them around to see shifts, shortages, surplus, and who wins and loses from a price control or a tax.

2A · Where the curves come from: willingness to pay

Each blue bar is one buyer’s willingness to pay (WTP) — the most they’d pay. Each orange bar is one seller’s minimum acceptable price (their cost). Line them up in order and you get the demand and supply staircases. Drag any bar top and drag the price line.

Things to try
  • Set the price very high. Nobody wants to buy — a surplus of sellers. Set it very low: a shortage. Notice how few trades happen either way.
  • Click “Go to equilibrium price”. Total surplus is at its maximum and deadweight loss is zero.
  • Raise buyer 4’s WTP above buyer 2’s? You can’t — bars stay sorted, just like a real demand curve slopes down.
  • Consumer surplus = WTP − price paid (dark blue). Producer surplus = price received − cost (dark orange).

2B · The market: shifts, equilibrium, surplus & deadweight loss

Drag the demand or supply curve to shift it (or use the sliders to change how steep it is). The dashed grey lines are your starting point, so you can see what changed. Then add a price ceiling, price floor, tax or subsidy and watch the surplus get shared out between consumers, producers, the government and deadweight loss.

Things to try
  • Shift vs. movement. Drag demand to the right: the curve shifts, and the market moves along the supply curve to a new equilibrium (price ↑, quantity ↑).
  • Drag supply to the left (e.g. a bad harvest): price ↑, quantity ↓ — the opposite quantity effect.
  • Shift both to the right. Quantity clearly rises — but price? It depends on which shift is bigger.
  • Price ceiling below equilibrium → a shortage, and the red DWL triangle shows trades that would have benefited both sides but no longer happen.
  • Tax: make demand steep (inelastic) and see who bears more of the burden — the side with the steeper curve. Watch the bar: part of the surplus moves to the government, part disappears as DWL.
  • Subsidy: buyers pay less and sellers get more, so both gain — but taxpayers foot a bigger bill than the gain. That gap is the DWL of over-production.

2C · Taxes & subsidies: who bears them, and who gets the surplus?

A tax drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep. Part of the old surplus becomes government revenue, part is lost as deadweight loss. A subsidy works the other way: buyers and sellers both gain, but taxpayers pay more than they gain. How the burden splits depends on substitution: the side that can more easily switch away (flatter curve) bears less. Slide the tax (left of zero = subsidy) and change how many substitutes each side has. Switch on Price breakdown to split the price into what the consumer pays and what the producer receives.

Things to try
  • Slide the tax up from zero and watch the bar: the government’s share grows, but so does deadweight loss — double the tax and DWL roughly quadruples.
  • Give buyers many substitutes (flat demand). Buyers just switch away, so sellers bear most of the tax and quantity falls a lot. With few substitutes, buyers bear most of it and the quantity barely moves — which is why governments tax inelastic goods (fuel, tobacco).
  • With Price breakdown on, compare the two dashed price lines. A tax makes the consumer pay more and the producer receive less than the original price; the gap between them is the tax. A subsidy does the opposite. The blue and orange bands show which side takes which part.
  • Set buyers’ and sellers’ substitutes equal: the burden splits 50/50 no matter who the law says pays.
  • Start with “Tax (net effect only)”: no curve shifts, you just see the wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers receive. Then choose “Tax to consumers (C)” (demand shifts down) or “Tax to producers (P)” (supply shifts up). The “market price” E* changes, but the quantity, what buyers pay, what sellers receive and the deadweight loss stay exactly the same. The same three choices exist for a subsidy.
  • Drag the slider below zero for a subsidy. Consumers and producers gain, but taxpayers pay more — the gap is deadweight loss from over-producing.

2D · Elasticity: how much does quantity react to price?

Elasticity = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price. On a straight-line demand curve it changes along the line: elastic (|ε| > 1) at high prices, inelastic (|ε| < 1) at low prices. That decides whether cutting price raises or lowers total revenue. Drag the dot along the demand curve.

Demand & revenue rectangle (P × Q)

Total revenue as quantity changes

Things to try
  • Start at the top of the demand curve and slide down. Revenue rises until |ε| = 1 (the midpoint), then falls.
  • Make demand flatter with the slider. The same price point now has a bigger |ε| — buyers are more responsive.
  • Why do firms selling necessities (insulin) or with few substitutes have pricing power? Because their demand is inelastic.

2E · The labour market: wage vs. hours, and how technology shifts supply (and demand)

Here the “good” is labour and its “price” is the wage. Workers supply hours (more hours are offered at a higher wage: the trade-off you met in Session 1C) and firms demand hours (each hour is worth its marginal product). Technology can shift either curve. Time-saving technology at home (appliances, delivery, remote work) frees hours and shifts supply to the right. Productivity technology makes each hour more valuable and shifts demand to the right, while automation that replaces workers shifts demand left. Use the sliders, or drag the curves.

Things to try
  • Push time-saving technology up. Supply shifts right: the wage falls but employment rises, and workers’ surplus changes as the wage drops.
  • Push productivity technology up instead. Demand shifts right: the wage and employment both rise.
  • Set productivity technology below zero (automation): the wage and employment both fall.
  • Move both sliders together. Employment rises, but the wage can go either way — it depends on which shift is bigger.

Cheat sheet: what shifts the curves?

Demand shifters

  • Income (normal vs. inferior goods)
  • Prices of substitutes & complements
  • Tastes & trends
  • Expectations about future prices
  • Number of buyers

A change in the good’s own price is a movement along the curve, not a shift.

Supply shifters

  • Input costs (wages, materials)
  • Technology (cheaper production shifts supply right)
  • Taxes & subsidies
  • Expectations
  • Number of sellers

What makes demand more elastic? More substitutes, luxuries, bigger share of budget, longer time horizon.

Key takeaways

  • Demand = buyers’ WTP; supply = sellers’ costs. The market equilibrium is where the two cross: quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • Above equilibrium price → surplus; below → shortage. Prices move to clear them.
  • CS is the area between the demand curve and price; PS the area between price and the supply curve. Equilibrium maximises total surplus.
  • Anything that stops trades that benefit both sides (ceiling, floor, tax) creates deadweight loss. A tax moves part of the surplus to the government; a subsidy “adds” surplus for buyers and sellers but costs taxpayers more than it adds.
  • In the labour market, the wage is the price of labour. Technology that saves workers’ time shifts supply right (wage ↓, hours ↑); productivity technology shifts demand right (wage ↑, hours ↑).
  • The more elastic side of the market bears less of a tax; elasticity also tells you if a price cut raises revenue.
